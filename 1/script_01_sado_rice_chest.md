### Script

**[0:00 - 0:30] HOOK**

In the summer of 1762, in the royal palace of Joseon Korea, a prince was dragged into a small wooden rice chest. The lid was shut. The lock was turned. And the man who ordered it... was his own father, the king.

For eight days, the prince remained inside. No food. No water. No escape.

This is not a legend. It's one of the most documented royal murders in Asian history — recorded in the official chronicles of the Joseon Dynasty. And the question that still haunts historians today is simple:

Was Crown Prince Sado truly insane... or was he the victim of a political conspiracy?

**[0:30 - 1:30] BACKGROUND**

To understand what happened, we need to go back to 1735. The Joseon Dynasty was at its peak. King Yeongjo ruled Korea with an iron fist and an obsession with order.

That year, a son was born. His name was Sado. He was the king's second son, but the first surviving male heir. From birth, he was destined to become Crown Prince — the future of the dynasty.

But destiny is rarely kind.

Sado's mother, Lady Yi, was a concubine of low status. In the rigid hierarchy of Joseon Korea, this mattered enormously. The queen consort, Queen Jeongseong, had no children. She was Sado's legal mother, but their relationship was cold and distant.

Meanwhile, King Yeongjo was a man of extremes. He was brilliant, but also paranoid, controlling, and deeply insecure about his own legitimacy. He had fought his way to the throne through political maneuvering, and he feared conspiracies everywhere.

This atmosphere of suspicion would define Sado's entire life.

**[1:30 - 4:00] THE RISE OF SADO**

As Crown Prince, Sado was expected to be perfect. He was educated in Confucian classics, trained in martial arts, and groomed for kingship from childhood.

But something was wrong.

Historical records describe Sado as a sensitive, artistic child who was terrified of his father. King Yeongjo was known for his harsh discipline. He would publicly humiliate officials, and he applied the same severity to his son.

Sado's biographers note that the prince began showing signs of psychological distress as a teenager. He would have violent outbursts. He was obsessed with clothing — changing outfits dozens of times a day. He became terrified of thunderstorms.

Some historians believe Sado suffered from what we would now recognize as schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. Others argue his behavior was a reaction to years of abuse and impossible expectations.

But in 18th-century Korea, mental illness was not understood. It was seen as a moral failing — a sign of weak character or even demonic possession.

King Yeongjo's response was to tighten control. He restricted Sado's movements, monitored his servants, and demanded increasingly strict adherence to Confucian rituals. The pressure was suffocating.

**[4:00 - 7:00] THE ESCALATION**

By his twenties, Sado's condition had deteriorated. Historical records from the period describe disturbing incidents:

He began having violent episodes. On one occasion, he killed a eunuch. On another, he attacked a servant. He became obsessed with death and would order servants to be executed for minor mistakes.

But here's where the story takes a darker turn. Some of these "incidents" may have been fabricated or exaggerated by Sado's political enemies at court.

In Joseon Korea, the Crown Prince was not just a person — he was a political faction. Sado had supporters and rivals. The rival faction, led by powerful officials, had every reason to paint him as insane. A mad prince could be removed. A sane prince with political ambitions was dangerous.

The most damning evidence against Sado came from his own wife, Lady Hyegyeong. Years later, she wrote a memoir — the "Hanjungnok" — that detailed Sado's madness in horrifying detail. She described him killing servants, having hallucinations, and terrorizing the palace.

But historians have questioned her account. Lady Hyegyeong wrote her memoir after Sado's death, when her son — Sado's son — had become king. Her memoir served a political purpose: it justified Sado's removal and legitimized her son's succession.

Was she telling the truth? Or was she protecting her son's throne by confirming the official narrative?

**[7:00 - 9:00] THE RICE CHEST**

By 1762, the situation had become untenable. King Yeongjo was aging. The court was divided. And Sado's behavior — real or exaggerated — had become impossible to ignore.

On July 4, 1762, King Yeongjo made his decision. He summoned Sado to the palace. In front of the court, he accused his son of madness and treason.

Sado was not given a trial. He was not given a chance to defend himself. Instead, he was led to a small wooden chest — a "du" — used for storing rice. It was barely large enough for a man to sit in.

The lid was closed. The lock was turned.

For eight days, Sado remained inside. No food. No water. The summer heat was brutal. On the eighth day, he died.

The official record states that Sado died of "illness" — a transparent euphemism. Everyone knew he had been executed by his father.

But here's the final twist. King Yeongjo did not execute Sado directly. He used a method that avoided spilling royal blood — a taboo in Confucian tradition. By locking Sado in a rice chest, the king could claim his son had died of "illness" rather than execution.

It was a legal fiction. But in Joseon Korea, legal fictions were how power worked.

**[9:00 - 10:00] CONCLUSION**

After Sado's death, his son — a young boy named Jeongjo — was named Crown Prince. He would eventually become one of Joseon's greatest kings, and he spent his reign rehabilitating his father's memory.

Jeongjo built a magnificent tomb for Sado. He commissioned the "Hwaseong Fortress" in Sado's honor. And he ensured that his father's name was cleared in official records.

But the question remains: Was Sado truly mad? Or was he a political victim?

Modern historians are divided. Some believe Sado genuinely suffered from mental illness and was dangerous. Others argue he was a victim of court intrigue — a sensitive man destroyed by an abusive father and political enemies.

What we know for certain is this: a prince was locked in a rice chest by his own father. He died after eight days of suffering. And the official record called it "illness."

The truth, as always in history, lies somewhere in between.

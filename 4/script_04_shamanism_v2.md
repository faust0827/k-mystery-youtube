## Script

### [0:00 – 0:40] COLD OPEN

In 1477, outside the gates of the Joseon capital, a group of shamans performed a ritual.

Someone reported it. The court asked who had ordered it.

The answer: the palace had. The king's own wet nurse and a royal eunuch had gone there to pray.

The king's response was a single line, recorded in the Annals: *"Do not do this again."*

That is the whole story of Joseon in one exchange. For five hundred years, this Confucian state outlawed shamanism, taxed it, exiled its practitioners, and wrote laws against it — and for five hundred years, the palace kept calling them back.

**[ON-SCREEN SOURCE CARD 1]**
성종실록 8년 11월 1일 (1477)
"都門外有禱祀者… 曰自內行之. 奉保夫人與內官往焉."
*"Outside the city gate there were people performing rites… They said it was done from within the palace. The royal wet nurse and a eunuch had gone there."*

---

### [0:40 – 2:10] THE LAW: GET OUT OF THE CAPITAL

Korean shamanism — *musok* — is older than Buddhism, Confucianism, or Christianity in Korea. Its practitioners are the *mudang*, usually women, who speak for spirits and perform the *gut*: drumming, dancing, and negotiation with the dead.

The Joseon dynasty was built on Neo-Confucianism, and it saw all of this as *eumsa* — "lewd rites." Not just wrong. Disorderly.

In 1431, the Censorate petitioned King Sejong with a complaint that sounds remarkably modern: shamans were living inside the capital, and noblewomen were visiting them constantly. Sick people were being gathered in their houses, spreading disease through the neighborhoods.

The fix was simple. Move them out.

Shamans were pushed beyond the city walls, into settlements that the records literally call *mugyeok-ri* — "shaman villages." The most famous stood outside the South Gate and along the Han River at Noryangjin.

They were also pushed down the social order. Shamans were classified as *sacheon* — lowborn, ranked alongside butchers, actors, and slaves. In the early dynasty, they and their descendants were barred from receiving state land.

**[ON-SCREEN SOURCE CARD 2]**
세종실록 13년 7월 17일 (1431)
"巫覡本不得雜處朝市, 於城外遙隔處, 稱爲巫覡里."
*"Shamans were never permitted to live among the markets of the capital; they were gathered far outside the walls, in places called shaman villages."*

---

### [2:10 – 3:40] THE EXCEPTION: THE STAR SHRINE

Here is where the story stops being a simple ban.

The palace had its own shamans. They were called *gungmu* — state shamans — and they were housed in an office inside the palace grounds: the *Seongsucheong*, the Star Shrine.

Their job was to pray for the royal family and to avert disaster. This was not a secret. It was an institution, with a budget and a staff.

And when the country needed rain, the state called the shamans.

In 1410, King Taejong gathered seventy shamans at Baegaksan to pray for rain. In 1436, King Sejong gave shamans rice to perform rain rites — and the record notes that the rain came. Again and again, the Annals show the same pattern: the Confucian state condemning shamanic ritual in public, and requesting it in private.

The Star Shrine survived for over a century. In 1503, King Yeonsangun — no friend of Confucian officials — stated plainly that the office of state shamans was ancient and should continue.

**[ON-SCREEN SOURCE CARD 3]**
연산군일기 9년 2월 (1503)
"성수청에 국무를 둔 것은 그 유래가 이미 오래되었다."
*"The placing of state shamans in the Star Shrine is a practice of long standing."*

---

### [3:40 – 5:10] THE CRACKDOWN THAT KEPT FAILING

In 1478, King Seongjong lost patience.

The shamans had been expelled from the capital, and then they had simply come back. The prohibition had loosened. They were practicing in the city again, openly, "without the slightest hesitation."

His order to the Censorate is one of the most revealing lines in the Annals: *"It has become the custom. The lewd rites flourish. The ignorant crowd flocks to them. It cannot be left unprohibited."*

Read that again. It is the sound of a state admitting that its law is not working.

So the laws got harsher. Shamans caught inside the capital were punished. Officials who failed to arrest them were dismissed. And the state began to tax them — the *muse* — partly to squeeze them out of business, partly because it needed the money. In 1464, the government ordered a register of every shaman, compiled every three years, so the tax could be collected properly. Later records list the rates: the chief state shaman paid nine *geun*, an ordinary shaman one.

And yet: in 1684, a shaman named Makrye was caught inside the palace, performing rituals — and wearing the king's own dragon robe. She was sentenced to exile on an island.

**[ON-SCREEN SOURCE CARD 5]**
숙종실록 10년 2월 21일 (1684)
"무녀가 궁 안에 들어와 도사한 것은 참으로 몹시 놀랍고 지극히 불경하다."
*"That a shaman entered the palace and performed rites there is deeply shocking and gravely irreverent."*

In 1743, King Yeongjo signed a law ordering any shaman who entered the palace in royal robes to be banished to a remote island.

**[ON-SCREEN SOURCE CARD 6]**
수교신보 (1743)
"무녀가 궐 안으로 들어와 곤룡포를 입고 신사를 설행하는 자는 절도에 정배한다."
*"Any shaman who enters the palace, wears the royal dragon robe, and performs rites shall be banished to a remote island."*

By then, the pattern was unmistakable. Every generation passed a new law against the same practice. You do not need a new law for something that has stopped happening.

**[ON-SCREEN SOURCE CARD 4]**
성종실록 9년 1월 20일 (1478)
"무격이 풍속을 이루고 음사가 크게 행해져서… 이미 모조리 성 밖으로 쫓아내게 하였으나 근년에 금법이 차츰 풀리어 도성 안에서 예전과 같이 자행하여."
*"Shamans have become the custom and the lewd rites flourish… We already drove them all outside the walls, but in recent years the prohibition has loosened and they practice in the capital as before."*

---

### [5:10 – 7:00] WHY IT NEVER WORKED

Because the ban was aimed at the wrong target.

The state could legislate where a shaman lived. It could not legislate why people went to one.

Consider what the 1431 petition actually complained about: noblewomen visiting shaman houses. Not peasants. Not the uneducated. The wives of the elite, slipping out of the capital to ask about a sick child, a difficult birth, a husband's career, a dead parent.

The Annals are full of this. A queen dowager calls for a shaman when the king falls ill. A royal concubine sends for one. A crown prince's illness becomes a shamanic ritual conducted by the palace itself.

And there was a practical reason shamans were hard to erase: they were also the medical system.

The state assigned shamans to the *Hwalinseo*, the public clinic for the poor, where they treated patients alongside physicians. The tax they paid helped fund it. For people with no access to a doctor, the shaman was not superstition. She was the only help available.

Grief, illness, infertility, sudden death — the things a Confucian bureaucracy had no ritual for. Shamanism had a ritual for all of them.

You can outlaw a practice. You cannot outlaw grief.

---

### [7:00 – 8:30] WHAT SURVIVED

By the eighteenth century, the state had stopped trying to win. In 1745, Yeongjo formally abolished the last official shamanic rites from the register of state ceremonies. In 1763, the Censorate asked once more to expel the shamans from Seoul, and the king agreed.

And still they came.

In the final decades of the dynasty, the most powerful woman in Korea — Queen Min, the future Empress Myeongseong — kept shamans close enough that two of them were granted honorary noble titles.

When Japan annexed Korea in 1910, the new colonial government tried again, classifying shamanism as primitive superstition. Then the military governments of the 1960s and 70s tried again, shutting down shrines and calling it backward.

Each time, the same result.

Today, shamanism is not a relic. It is a working profession. Mudangs counsel clients, perform *gut* for the dead, and appear on television. Estimates of their number range wildly — from tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands — but the more telling fact is cultural: when a Korean film wants to talk about grief, guilt, and the unburied dead, it reaches for the shaman.

*The Wailing* in 2016. *Exhuma* in 2024.

Both films are built on concepts that the Joseon state spent five hundred years trying to erase: *sal*, spiritual contamination; *wonhon*, the restless dead; and *sinbyeong*, the spirit sickness that is said to choose a shaman whether she wants it or not.

---

### [8:30 – 9:40] THE PRICE OF BEING CHOSEN

Which brings us back to the thing the laws could never touch.

In the tradition, a mudang does not decide to become one. The spirits decide. The sign is *sinbyeong*: unexplained illness, seizures, hallucinations, nightmares — a body that medicine cannot fix.

The cure is *naerim*, an initiation ritual in which the person accepts the spirit. Refuse, the tradition says, and the illness worsens. Some accounts say people died.

Modern psychiatry has another name for parts of this. It also has patients.

That tension — illness or calling, diagnosis or destiny — is why the story never resolves. The state could classify shamans as lowborn. It could tax them, exile them, and write them out of official ceremony.

It could not answer the question they were being asked.

---

### [9:40 – 10:00] CLOSE

Five hundred years of laws. Five hundred years of the same ritual, performed just outside the gate.

The kings of Joseon were not fighting a superstition. They were fighting the fact that, in the dark, when someone is dying or gone, people want someone to speak to the other side.

The state had laws. The shamans had an answer.

**[ON-SCREEN CLOSE CARD]**
"무격이 풍속을 이루고…" — 성종실록, 1478
*Some things are outlawed for centuries precisely because they cannot be outlawed.*

By the eighteenth century, the state had stopped trying to win. In 1745, Yeongjo formally abolished the last official shamanic rites from the register of state ceremonies. In 1763, the Censorate asked once more to expel the shamans from Seoul, and the king agreed.

And still they came.

In the final decades of the dynasty, the most powerful woman in Korea, Queen Min, the future Empress Myeongseong, kept shamans close enough that two of them were granted honorary noble titles.

When Japan annexed Korea in 1910, the new colonial government tried again, classifying shamanism as primitive superstition. Then the military governments of the 1960s and seventies tried again, shutting down shrines and calling it backward.

Each time, the same result.

Today, shamanism is not a relic. It is a working profession. Mudangs counsel clients, perform gut for the dead, and appear on television. Estimates of their number range wildly, from tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands. But the more telling fact is cultural. When a Korean film wants to talk about grief, guilt, and the unburied dead, it reaches for the shaman.

The Wailing, in 2016. Exhuma, in 2024.

Both films are built on concepts that the Joseon state spent five hundred years trying to erase. Sal, spiritual contamination. Wonhon, the restless dead. And sinbyeong, the spirit sickness that is said to choose a shaman whether she wants it or not.

Because the ban was aimed at the wrong target.

The state could legislate where a shaman lived. It could not legislate why people went to one.

Consider what the 1431 petition actually complained about. Noblewomen visiting shaman houses. Not peasants. Not the uneducated. The wives of the elite, slipping out of the capital to ask about a sick child, a difficult birth, a husband's career, a dead parent.

The Annals are full of this. A queen dowager calls for a shaman when the king falls ill. A royal concubine sends for one. A crown prince's illness becomes a shamanic ritual conducted by the palace itself.

And there was a practical reason shamans were hard to erase. They were also the medical system.

The state assigned shamans to the Hwalinseo, the public clinic for the poor, where they treated patients alongside physicians. The tax they paid helped fund it. For people with no access to a doctor, the shaman was not superstition. She was the only help available.

Grief, illness, infertility, sudden death. The things a Confucian bureaucracy had no ritual for. Shamanism had a ritual for all of them.

You can outlaw a practice. You cannot outlaw grief.

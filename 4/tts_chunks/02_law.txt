Korean shamanism, musok, is older than Buddhism, Confucianism, or Christianity in Korea. Its practitioners are the mudang, usually women, who speak for spirits and perform the gut: drumming, dancing, and negotiation with the dead.

The Joseon dynasty was built on Neo-Confucianism, and it saw all of this as eumsa. Lewd rites. Not just wrong. Disorderly.

In 1431, the Censorate petitioned King Sejong with a complaint that sounds remarkably modern. Shamans were living inside the capital, and noblewomen were visiting them constantly. Sick people were being gathered in their houses, spreading disease through the neighborhoods.

The fix was simple. Move them out.

Shamans were pushed beyond the city walls, into settlements that the records literally call mugyeok-ri. Shaman villages. The most famous stood outside the South Gate, and along the Han River at Noryangjin.

They were also pushed down the social order. Shamans were classified as sacheon. Lowborn, ranked alongside butchers, actors, and slaves. In the early dynasty, they and their descendants were barred from receiving state land.
